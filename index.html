Bet type

Goals bets: Over or Under total goals in the match. One bet per game, 1 unit each.

Double chance: covers two of the three results: home or draw (1X), draw or away (X2), home or away (12). Shorter prices, so they need to win more often.

Which line

Main picks: the standard bet on each game, almost always Over or Under 2.5 (a few are a 3.5 line or a team total). This is the headline record.

Alt (3.5 goals): the same game's Over or Under 3.5 bet. Tracked separately so it never mixes into the main record.

Alt (1.5 goals): appears here once we start logging Over or Under 1.5 bets with a price. None have been logged yet.

Near misses: games that almost met our rules, just under the cutoff. Tracked only to learn if the cutoffs are too strict. Not part of the main record.

Over or Under

Record: wins and losses among played bets.

Hit rate: wins divided by played bets.

Units: profit if every bet risked 1 unit at the listed odds. A win pays the odds, a loss costs 1.

ROI: units won per unit risked.

Current vs Filtered model

What this compares. Both tracks start from the same list of games. Nothing here is played with real money. We are only collecting results.

Current model is the method we run today: every game on the list is eligible and nothing is filtered out.

Filtered model is the same model with four extra rules switched on to see if they help: skip Under bets in the Premier League and LaLiga 2, and build slips with one leg per league, no two legs priced -300 or shorter, and no leg where the model's edge over the book is above 10 points or below zero.

The aim is to learn whether cutting the weaker leagues and the riskiest slip legs makes the results better over a larger sample.

Models (independent)

Data collection only. Nothing here touches any pick, slip, record or track.

What this is. Several simple models predict every game's chance of finishing Under 2.5 and Under 3.5. When the game ends we score each one. They are never used to choose a bet, and nothing on the other tabs reads from them.

Brier score measures how close the chances were to what happened: 0 is perfect and 0.25 is what you get by always saying 50%. Lower is better. At this sample size the gaps between models are noise, so the plan is to keep collecting and compare trends once there are enough games.

Inputs. Own data models use the league record or the Bet365 price, nothing from the outlook data. Outlook total models all start from the projected total and only differ in how they spread goals around it, so they will look alike. The outlook chance is shown for reference.

Over versus Under. Over is the mirror of Under on the same line: the chance of Over 2.5 is 100% minus the chance of Under 2.5, and the same for 3.5. So the four buttons are two sets of predictions shown from both sides. The chances and the green and red marks flip, but the Brier scores are identical for Under 2.5 and Over 2.5, and for Under 3.5 and Over 3.5. The 1.5 line is tracked for the models that need no price (league rate, Poisson, negative binomial, bivariate Poisson, Markov and the blend). The Bet365 price model needs a logged 1.5 price, so it starts when we log some. The 4.5 line is not tracked.

Shared games are games where every model had a number, so the comparison is fair. League rates only exist for nine leagues, which is why that count is smaller.

Each finished game adds one more example for every model. With only a few dozen games, a model can look good or bad by luck, so the scores wobble a lot from day to day. The bar counts finished games toward a target of 300, which is roughly where a gap of a hundredth or two in Brier score starts to mean something. We keep collecting until then and only compare trends after that.

The 300 is my starting guess and can be changed in the data file if you want a different goal.

Games is how many finished games that model had a chance for. The models need different things: the league model needs a league with a known rate, the Bet365 price model needs a price we logged at that line, and the others need the projected total. A model that covers fewer games is scored on fewer games, so compare the Shared column when you want a fair fight.

Brier score grades a chance after the game is over. If a model said 70% Under and the game ended Under, the miss is 0.30 and the penalty is 0.30 x 0.30 = 0.09. If it ended Over instead, the miss is 0.70 and the penalty is 0.49. The score is the average penalty over all games.

Lower is better. 0 would be perfect, and a higher number means worse. Confident chances that turn out right score very low, confident chances that turn out wrong are punished hard, and hedging near 50% always lands in the middle.

Two yardsticks sit at the bottom of the table. "Always 50%" scores exactly 0.25. "Always the average" says the same chance for every game, namely the share of games that really ended under the line, and it uses hindsight. A model only shows real skill if it scores below that second row. This matters most for Under 3.5, where most games end under, so a model can sit far below 0.25 and still be doing no better than the average.

What a good score looks like. As far as I know, Over/Under lines are close to coin flips, so even strong models usually finish only a little below the "always the average" row. A gap of a hundredth or so is already notable, and with this few games it can easily be luck.

What it does not tell you. Brier says how accurate the chances are. It does not say a bet is good, and there is no score range that means "confident enough to bet". A model with a great score can still lose money if the price already reflects what it knows. That is why the Bet365 price model is the benchmark to watch. We are not turning any of this into a rule until the sample is big enough.

The gold number only marks the lowest score in that column right now.

Shared is the same Brier score, but only on games where every model had a chance. That way every row is graded on the exact same games, which makes it a fair comparison. The catch is that this set is smaller (the league model only covers nine leagues), so the numbers are noisier than the main Brier column. The count of shared games is in the note under the table.
